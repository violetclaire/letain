Beauty Reporter

Counter Intelligence

Filed this month from the fragrance floor, the manicure table, and one very defensive press office.

New York · Nails

The Little Black Bottle

Chanel's Vamp — a red so dark it reads as dried blood, which is apparently a compliment now — is sold out at every counter we called. One saleswoman told us the waiting list "is not a list anymore, it is a manuscript." Copycat formulas are already circling; none of them have the nerve.

Rouge Noir nail lacquer, $15 — if you can find it
New York · Fragrance

One Scent, Everybody In

The new thing in fragrance is that it isn't for you specifically. CK One is marketed to him, to her, and to whoever is borrowing whose flannel this week. It smells like clean laundry and shared apartments. We predict every teenager in America owns it by Christmas.

CK One, 3.4 oz., $35
Cambridge · Skin

The Acid Test

Alpha hydroxy acids have completed their journey from dermatology journals to drugstore shelves in under three years. Do they erase a decade? A dermatologist we trust says they erase "a good winter." Everyone is exfoliating. No one is moisturizing enough. Full report on page 64 of our September issue.

Glycolic creams, $8 to $60, results identical
Los Angeles · Lips

Lipstick That Stays Put

Revlon's new ColorStay does exactly what it threatens: it does not move. Not onto coffee cups, not onto collars, not onto anyone else. Our tester wore Toast of New York through a salad, a screening, and an argument. The color outlasted the relationship.

ColorStay lipcolor, $5.95 at drugstores
Everywhere · Brows

An Eyebrow Bulletin

Brows have gotten thin. Alarmingly thin. Tweezed-by-flashlight thin. We are contractually obligated to remind you that brows are a renewable resource only up to a point, and that point was last Tuesday. Put down the tweezers and back away from the magnifying mirror.

Restraint, free
Paris · Forecast

Matte Is the Message

The runways have spoken and they said nothing shiny. Skin is powdered flat, lids are taupe, mouths are brown, and the entire effect suggests a woman too busy to glisten. Shimmer will be back — it always comes back — but for now, matte is a moral position.

Loose powder, translucent, apply with conviction

Special Report

The Brown Study

Two years ago a brown mouth meant something had gone wrong with your lipstick. Today it means everything has gone right with your life. An investigation.

By Marguerite Ossining · Swatches by the Art Department

Consider the American mouth, circa 1989: it was red, or it was pink, or it was frosted into something resembling a bathroom fixture, and it apologized for nothing. Then consider it now, in the summer of 1994: it is the color of coffee with not quite enough milk, of a paper bag, of toast — Toast of New York, to be exact, a shade name that sounds like a sandwich and sells like a controlled substance.

What happened in between was, depending on whom you ask, grunge, a recession, Kate Moss, or the collective realization that looking as if you were trying had become the one unforgivable social error. Brown lipstick is the mouth that insists it woke up like this — never mind that achieving it requires a lip pencil, a blotting ritual, and a small monthly income.

"Red says look at me. Brown says I know you're already looking, and I'm choosing not to care."

"Red says look at me," a makeup artist told us backstage in Bryant Park, wielding a pencil the color of cinnamon. "Brown says I know you're already looking, and I'm choosing not to care." She then spent eleven minutes on a mouth that was meant to look like eleven seconds.

The numbers back the mood. Counter staff report that anything named after a foodstuff — Spice, Toast, Cappuccino, Gingerbread — is on allocation. One buyer told us her brown-family sales have tripled since spring, "and the reds are just sitting there, sulking." Even the nail world has gone dark: Chanel's Vamp, a red-black the house describes as "cerise noir" and everyone else describes as "arterial," is the hardest-to-get fifteen dollars in America (see Beauty Reporter, page 23).

Will it last? Nothing lasts; that is the entire business model. But brown has done something red never quite managed: it made minimalism look like a decision instead of a surrender. Somewhere in a lab in New Jersey, someone is already mixing the shade that kills it. Our money says it will be beige, it will be called something like Vapor, and you will buy three.
